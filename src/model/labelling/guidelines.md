# Guidelines for Labeling

> prompt_version: 1.1 (Bump up on every change to the guidelines)

These guidelines are intended to help ensure consistency and accuracy in the labeling of consumer complaints about
financial products (credit reports, debt collection, mortgages, bank accounts, cards, loans and money transfers). Each
complaint gets two labels: an **urgency** level and a **sentiment**.

## Quick Notes

Some complaints may have masked text, XXXX or redacted information, [REDACTED], or other placeholders. Please do not
let this affect your labeling decisions. Masked text hides personal details such as names, dates and account numbers;
it says nothing about how urgent the complaint is or how the consumer feels. Base both labels only on the information
provided in the complaint, regardless of any redactions.

## Urgency

Urgency is about the **consumer's situation**: how soon they face real harm (losing money, losing access to their
money, losing their home or car, or legal trouble) if nothing is done. Judge the situation described, not how strongly
the consumer words it.

1. **Critical**: Serious harm is happening now or is about to happen. Immediate attention required.
   **Examples Include:** Fraud or identity theft happening now, account locked or funds frozen, foreclosure or
   repossession about to happen, legal threats.

2. **High**: The consumer has already been harmed, or will be soon unless someone acts. Prompt attention required.
   **Examples Include:** Money already lost, a deadline coming up, repeated failed contact.

3. **Medium**: Something is wrong and needs fixing, but there is no immediate harm. **Examples Include:** A wrong
   charge or reporting error with no immediate harm.

4. **Low**: Nothing is at stake beyond general dissatisfaction. These can be addressed in due course. **Examples
   Include:** General inquiries or requests for information, complaints about poor service with no financial impact.

## Sentiment

Sentiment is about the **tone** of the complaint: how the consumer feels, as shown by the way they write.

1. **Negative**: The consumer expresses frustration, anger, distress or worry. Most complaints are negative.

2. **Neutral**: The complaint states facts or asks for something without expressing feelings either way.

3. **Positive**: The consumer expresses satisfaction or gratitude, for example thanking someone who resolved part of
   the problem. This will be rare.

## Tie-Breaking Rules

When a complaint could reasonably be assigned to more than one urgency level, the following rules should be applied:

1. If the issue has a significant financial impact, it should be assigned a higher urgency level.
2. If the issue involves fraud, identity theft or account security, it should be assigned a higher urgency level.
3. If the consumer faces a deadline or legal action, it should be assigned a higher urgency level.
4. If the levels are still tied, choose the higher of the two.

When a complaint mixes tones (for example, thanks for one thing but anger about another), choose the sentiment that
dominates the complaint as a whole.
